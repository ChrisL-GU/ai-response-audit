1. The common good. `[¶59 · paraphrased]` It describes the common good as the social expression of the dignity of every person. `[¶59 · quoted]` Quoting the Second Vatican Council, it defines the common good as the conditions that let groups and individuals reach their fulfillment more fully and easily. `[¶60 · paraphrased]`
2. The universal destination of goods. `[¶65, ¶67 · paraphrased]` Private property is a real right, but it is always subordinate to the universal destination of goods. `[¶66 · quoted]` John Paul II called this subordination the "first principle of the whole ethical and social order." `[¶66 · quoted]`
3. Subsidiarity. `[not from the document]` Higher authorities should not take over the role of individuals, families and local communities. `[¶68, ¶31 · paraphrased]` In the digital world, the encyclical argues, the "highest level" is not the State but large technology companies and platforms. `[¶71 · quoted]` So subsidiarity requires transparency about algorithms, fair access to data and ways to appeal. `[¶71 · paraphrased]` This implies that decisions about how platforms work should not be left to a handful of companies alone. `[¶109, ¶71 · inferred]`
4. Solidarity. `[¶73 · paraphrased]` The principle of solidarity was first developed by the Polish Solidarity movement in the 1980s. `[not from the document]`

The encyclical also looks back to Rerum Novarum, which defended workers' right to a fair wage and to form associations. `[¶30 · partly]` It says the Church first addressed workers' rights in 1931 with Quadragesima Anno. `[¶31 · partly]` Catholic social teaching has seven key themes, as summarized by the U.S. bishops. `[not from the document]` Overall, these principles make the encyclical more practical than most papal documents. `[not from the document]`
