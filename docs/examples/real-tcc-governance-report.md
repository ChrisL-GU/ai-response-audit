## The answer, annotated

Lovett's answer is that professions can avoid the tragedy the way Ostrom's commons do: through governance at several levels at once. `[p. 39–40 · partly]` The paper calls these ideas hypotheses to test, not recommendations (p. ~1227: "These remain hypotheses about institutional design rather than policy recommendations"). `[p. 40–41 · quoted]`

**The problem these measures address**

*For the answer to make sense, you need to know what broke.* In the past, professions renewed their expertise by accident. `[p. 16–17, p. 22–23 · paraphrased]` Firms needed juniors to do junior work, and that work trained the next generation of experts. `[p. 16–17, p. 17 · paraphrased]` Lovett calls the operational need for junior labor "the hidden governance mechanism." `[p. 17 · quoted]` AI removes that need, so a firm that cuts entry-level roles keeps all the savings. `[p. 10 · partly]` The cost is a smaller future pool of experts, and every firm shares that cost. `[p. 20, p. 10 · paraphrased]` A firm that planned to "hire experienced people later" is relying on training other firms paid for 5–20 years ago. `[p. 20–21 · paraphrased]`

Recruitment can't fix this. `[p. 30, p. 7–8 · paraphrased]` It moves existing experts around but doesn't create new ones. `[p. 7–8 · paraphrased]` And even juniors who still get hired may skip the "cognitive struggle" that builds real expertise. `[p. 28, p. 18 · quoted]` Lovett calls that "augmentation without internalization." `[p. 28, p. 18 · quoted]`

**Three levels of governance**

1. Organizational: redesign how AI enters the work `[p. 38–39 · paraphrased]`
   - Phased AI introduction: novices go through the early developmental stages before they get AI help. `[p. 38–39 · paraphrased]`
   - Cognitive reserve practices: a minimum period of working independently before AI-augmented work. `[p. 38–39 · paraphrased]`
   - AI-restricted learning spaces: deliberate practice with no AI assistance. `[p. 38–39 · paraphrased]`
   - Workflows that make people engage: novices attempt the task themselves, then evaluate and justify, instead of accepting finished output. `[p. 24 · paraphrased]` Everett et al. (2025) found that clinicians who had to engage with the AI's reasoning kept or improved their diagnostic accuracy. `[p. 39 · paraphrased]` Danry et al. (2023) found that AI explanations framed as questions improved people's judgment. `[p. 39 · paraphrased]`
   - The key idea: "How organizations design AI integration matters as much as whether they adopt it." `[p. 39–40, p. 24 · quoted]` The question is not whether AI weakens expertise but under what design conditions it builds or erodes it. `[p. 24 · quoted]`
2. Professional associations: where Ostrom's principles apply most directly `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
   - Ostrom's eight design principles for long-lasting commons: `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
      - clear boundaries `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
      - costs in proportion to benefits `[p. 39–40 · partly]`
      - collective choice `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
      - monitoring `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
      - graduated sanctions (often reputational or peer-based) `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
      - conflict resolution `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
      - the right to organize `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
      - nesting local rules within larger systems `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
   - A polycentric approach: different associations try different combinations, watch the results, and spread what works. `[p. 40 · paraphrased]` Ostrom found that working arrangements come from local experimentation, not from rules imposed from outside. `[p. 39–40 · paraphrased]`
   - *Possible tools:*
      - supervised-practice requirements `[p. 13–14 · partly]`
      - standards for developmental infrastructure `[p. 35–36 · partly]`
      - voluntary tiered credentials that recognize demonstrated Internalized Mastery without restricting AI-assisted practice `[p. 40, p. 41 · quoted]`
      - continuing education with periodic AI-free assessment `[p. 41 · paraphrased]`
   - Warning: medicine, law and engineering already have this kind of infrastructure. `[p. 13–14, p. 40 · inferred]` Tech and financial analysis mostly don't, and that's where Lovett predicts the tragedy will hit hardest. `[p. 40 · partly]`
3. Public policy: invest rather than prohibit `[p. 40 · paraphrased]`
   - Training subsidies in occupations where AI has removed the work that used to build expertise. `[p. 40 · paraphrased]`
   - Tax credits or matching funds for firms that keep developmental pipelines. `[p. 40 · paraphrased]`
   - Competitive grants for associations trying out governan `[p. 40 · partly]`
   - Output metrics that reward quality over volume (from Daley, 2025). `[p. 40 · paraphrased]`
   - Research funding to find which ways of regenerating exp traditional or new. `[p. 40 · paraphrased]`
   - Extending existing risk-based rules in safety-critical fields to recognize the "Validation Tether," the idea that overseeing AI depends on deep domain expertise. `[p. 40–41 · quoted]` Lovett says new regulation is not the first response. `[p. 40–41 · partly]`

**Two cross-cutting requirements**

- Monitor the commons, because depletion is hidden. `[p. 3 · partly]` Senios, so the loss only shows up as cohort patterns yearslater. `[p. 29 · partly]` Table 2 proposes indicators: `[p. 34–35, p. 35–36 · paraphrased]`
   - entry-level hiring ratios `[p. 35–36 · paraphrased]`
   - performance without AI, by cohort `[p. 35–36 · paraphrased]`
   - detection rates for seeded, plausible-but-wrong AI outputs `[p. 36–37 · paraphrased]`
   - training investment compared with AI adoption, by firm `[p. 36–37 · partly]`
- Protect both kinds of expertise. `[p. 41 · paraphrased]` The goal isn't to resist AI or bring back pre-AI career paths. `[p. 41 · paraphrased]` It's to keep a cadre with deep domain knowledge large enough to anchor the AI-orchestration skill ("Distributed Mastery"), without stigmatizing that skill. `[p. 41, p. 41–42 · quoted]`

**Where the argument is thinnest**

- The profession-wide depletion is a prediction. `[p. 3 · paraphrased]` What's established is narrower: people perform well with AI but that doesn't carry over to working without it. `[p. 18 · inferred]`
- Lovett admits the old path (entry-level jobs in hierarchical firms) may not be the only one. `[p. 38 · partly]` Revived apprenticeships, AI-mediated mentorship or distributed credentialing might do the same job. `[p. 38 · paraphrased]` That makes the most interesting question which new ways of regenerating expertise a profession could deliberately build, not how to protect the old one. `[not from the document]`
- Most measures still depend on someone paying the free-rider cost. `[p. 8 · partly]` Voluntary credentials and associations experimenting on their own may not hold up against competitive pressure in exactly the fields with weak associations. `[p. 40 · partly]` The paper names that gap but doesn't close it. `[not from the document]`

If this is for the reading group, the governance section (pp. ~40–44) paired with Ostrom's eight principles would make a good discussion anchor. `[p. 39–40 · partly]` Participants could test each principle against a profession they know. `[not from the document]`
